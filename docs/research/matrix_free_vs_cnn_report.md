# Logic and lookup models close the CNN gap

**Matrix-free, multiplier-free models already match a tiny CNN on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, but not on CIFAR-10, and the evidence behind that claim is thinner than it looks.** Convolutional Tsetlin machines (TMs) report about 99.1-99.4% on MNIST and 90-92.5% on Fashion-MNIST, against 92.5% for the best "under 100K parameter" CNN in the official benchmark table ([pyTsetlinMachine](https://github.com/cair/pyTsetlinMachine), [Drop Clause CTM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.14506), [Fashion-MNIST table](https://github.com/zalandoresearch/fashion-mnist)). Flat lookup-table networks (DWN, ULEEN) stall near 98.3% MNIST, 89% Fashion-MNIST and 57.5% CIFAR-10 ([DWN](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11112)). The only matrix-free routes near CNN accuracy on CIFAR-10 are convolutional logic-gate networks (86.29% with 61M gates) and Tsetlin composites (82.8%), and both depend on convolution-style scans and very large gate or clause counts ([Conv DLGN](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04732), [TM Composites](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.04801)). Beating a full-size GPU CNN (the best Fashion-MNIST entry is 96.7%) is not supported by any source read. Shirt versus T-shirt is hard mainly because the classes overlap by definition, not because of random label noise. **Source warning:** the research sessions were blocked from arxiv.org, ar5iv, OpenReview, NeurIPS, huggingface.co, mlcommons.org, iea.org and several other hosts. No paper table was read directly. Nearly every number below is second-hand (a search-engine summary of a paper, a README, or a vendor page) and is flagged as such; the final section lists what to re-check before any number goes on a slide.

## What counts as verified in this report

Only a few sources were read in full: GitHub READMEs and documentation pages. These include the Fashion-MNIST benchmark table, pyTsetlinMachine, PyTsetlinMachineCUDA, the original TsetlinMachine repo, the DWN repo and example, the difflogic repo, and the BitNetMCU documentation. Numbers from these are "verified from primary" in the sense that the author's own page states them, although the Fashion-MNIST table is submitted by users and the maintainers say they have not tested the entries ([README](https://github.com/zalandoresearch/fashion-mnist)). Everything else is second-hand: a search summary of an arXiv paper, a company blog, a press release, a patent that reproduces Horowitz's table, or a review site. Where I write "second-hand", the paper exists and was identified, but its table was not opened. The notes also contain a few figures labelled UNVERIFIED-MEMORY (for example XNOR-Net ImageNet numbers, a "greater than 95%" float-CNN CIFAR-10 figure, HTM MNIST accuracy); I omit or flag them.

One citation error in the brief is worth correcting: arXiv 2203.01479 is BTHOWeN (Susskind et al.), not ULEEN; ULEEN is arXiv 2304.10618 (notes, weightless file).

## Tsetlin machines and logic networks have the strongest accuracy evidence

The pattern is clear. On MNIST the TM and the best LUT nets are within 1 point of a 99.2-99.3% tiny CNN ([Fashion-MNIST table](https://github.com/zalandoresearch/fashion-mnist)). On Fashion-MNIST the convolutional TM family sits at or just above the tiny-CNN line (92.5%), with the newest variants at 93-94.7% in a single 2025 paper that I could not read. Flat LUT networks are about 2 to 3 points short. On CIFAR-10 everything flat collapses to about 57%, while anything with local patches and shared structure climbs into the 75-86% range.

### How the leading methods learn and what inference needs

Tsetlin machines learn without gradients. They learn online, one example at a time, using Type I and Type II feedback on Tsetlin automata. Inference is a majority vote over conjunctive clauses, so it needs bitwise AND over included literals, a count, and integer adds; weighted and coalesced variants add integer clause weights to the votes ([TsetlinMachine README](https://github.com/cair/TsetlinMachine), [tmu](https://github.com/cair/tmu)). Inputs must be Boolean: MNIST is thresholded (at 75) and Fashion-MNIST uses adaptive Gaussian thresholding ([PyTsetlinMachineCUDA](https://github.com/cair/PyTsetlinMachineCUDA)). Differentiable logic-gate networks (DLGN) are the opposite case: they train with Adam and backpropagation on a GPU through a continuous relaxation that picks one of 16 two-input gates per neuron, then discretise to pure gate circuits whose only arithmetic is a final popcount ("GroupSum"). They compile to C and exceed one million MNIST images per second on one CPU core ([difflogic](https://github.com/Felix-Petersen/difflogic)). DWN trains on a GPU with an extended finite-difference gradient for binary LUT contents; its MNIST example uses thermometer-encoded inputs, two LUT layers (2,000 and 1,000 LUTs, 6 inputs each) and a GroupSum output ([DWN repo](https://github.com/alanbacellar/DWN)). All of these fit the brief's "train on a GPU, infer on chip" split. The inference ops for the TM and DLGN are derived from the descriptions above; no source gave exact per-inference operation counts for the TM.

### Why CIFAR-10 is the decisive test

The jump from 62% to 86% in logic-gate networks came from architecture, not from more training. The 2022 DLGN reached about 62.14% on CIFAR-10 ([review of 2210.08277](https://liner.com/review/deep-differentiable-logic-gate-networks)). The 2024 convolutional version added logic-gate tree convolutions, logical OR pooling and residual initialisation. It reports 86.29% with 61M gates, and its tables compare against FINN CNV (80.10%, 901M gates), LUTNet (84.95%, 1,290M gates) and TTNet large (70.75%, 189M gates) ([arXiv HTML 2411.04732](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04732v1)). A SlidesLive page lists 86.38% and 111M gates, which conflicts with the paper; prefer the paper's figures but re-check ([SlidesLive](https://slideslive.com/39028046/convolutional-differentiable-logic-gate-networks)). The same reasoning applies to the weightless line: reaching 80%+ on CIFAR-10 appears to need convolution-like structure or residual binarisation, and an FPL 2025 hybrid-WNN paper claims 87.72% but was seen only as a snippet, so its architecture and memory are unknown ([FPL 2025](https://utw10244.utweb.utexas.edu/pubs/FPL_2025_WNN.pdf)). The implication for a lookup-only chip is that "no matrix" cannot also mean "no weight sharing": a sliding-window scan with shared tables is allowed by the founder's rule (scans and table lookups are permitted), whereas a dense matrix-vector product is not. A 61M-gate model is also large for an on-chip block, so gate or table count must be reported next to accuracy.

## Binary nets, hashing and local learning show where the gap comes from

Binary and ternary nets lose little accuracy but fail the matrix-free rule. Binary-weight and binary-activation networks report 0.96% MNIST error and 11.40% CIFAR-10 error (Theano version), and BinaryConnect 1.29% and 9.90% ([BNN paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.02830), second-hand). BitNetMCU is the best-documented memory data point: a ternary fully-connected net with 93,568 bits (11.42 KB) reaches 99.45% on 16x16 downscaled MNIST, a 4-bit net of 12.3 KB reaches 99.02%, and rotation/affine augmentation was the main accuracy factor; it has no Fashion-MNIST or CIFAR-10 result ([BitNetMCU docs](https://github.com/cpldcpu/BitNetMCU/blob/main/docs/documentation.md), primary). But these are still matrix-vector products with add-only arithmetic, so they remove multiplies and not the matrix; the notes make the same point. No 1-4 bit Fashion-MNIST accuracy with memory in KB was found anywhere, so the cost of binarising the 92.5% tiny CNN is unmeasured.

Hashing and random-feature readouts are weak as classifiers. FlyHash and BioHash are evaluated for retrieval (mAP), and no verified Fashion-MNIST classification accuracy exists for FlyHash, BioHash or HTM ([FlyHash](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/180471.full.pdf), [BioHash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04907)). A 2026 connectome study found Gaussian projections with equal arithmetic retrieve better than fly hashing on every dataset tested, except at very short hash lengths ([alphaxiv 2610.09114](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.09114v1)). The notable positive result is Coates, Lee and Ng: a single layer of k-means features on whitened, densely extracted patches plus a linear readout reached **79.6% on CIFAR-10**, and width and dense stride mattered more than the learning algorithm ([AISTATS 2011](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v15/coates11a.html)). An informal forum post reports an extreme-learning-machine readout at 94.12% MNIST with 1,000 hidden units and 97.66% with 10,000 (unverified) ([PyTorch forum](https://discuss.pytorch.org/t/basic-extreme-learning-machine-pytorch-implementation/200562)).

Backprop-free local learning is strong on MNIST and still behind on harder data. SoftHebb (multi-layer, convolutional, Hebbian soft winner-take-all, trained linear readout) reports 99.4% MNIST, 80.3% CIFAR-10, 76.2% STL-10 and 27.3% ImageNet; a single layer gets 71.10% on CIFAR-10 versus 62.69% for hard WTA and 63.93% for random weights ([SoftHebb](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.11883)). On Fashion-MNIST the backprop-free numbers are poor: about 87.1% for a sparse-goodness Forward-Forward variant, about 84% for supervised Hebbian learning, and 75.14% for shallow SoftHebb ([arXiv 2501.09238](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09238), [2203.01304](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.01304), [2209.11883](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.11883)). Plain Forward-Forward is weak on CIFAR-10 (about 56% in reproductions) ([arXiv 2504.21662](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.21662)). The most useful inference is a hypothesis, not a finding: HOG features with an SVM reach 92.6% on Fashion-MNIST with no backpropagation, which equals the 92.5% tiny CNN ([Fashion-MNIST table](https://github.com/zalandoresearch/fashion-mnist), primary). A fixed, edge-oriented, spatially pooled feature bank with a counting or lookup readout is therefore the cheapest route to CNN-level Fashion-MNIST accuracy, but nobody has tested it with a counting readout. The notes also record the repo's own earlier finding that a 5-bit table loses about 6 points against a float readout (the repo's note; I could not verify it).

## Why Shirt and T-shirt are hard, and what is known about fixing it

Shirt is the worst class in every model examined, but nothing read quantifies it for a strong model. Weak or student models (87-90% overall) get Shirt recall of 61-80%: one blog CNN gets 67.7%, with 98 Shirts predicted as T-shirt/top and 103 as Coat; a VGG16-based model sends 104 Shirts to T-shirt, 52 to Pullover and 38 to Coat; Berkeley course projects report 61% to 80% ([hashnode](https://hashnode.com/posts/neural-network-from-scratch-mnist-fashion-mnist/6a9a81636e269aa076faab76), [arXiv 2603.10731](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.10731), [Berkeley projects](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs194-26/sp20/upload/files/proj4/cs194-26-afk/)). These are all second-hand, and they describe weak models, so they show the confusion pattern and not a ceiling. Two papers attribute the confusion to category ambiguity: capsule-network error analysis calls some T-shirt/top versus Shirt mistakes ambiguous to humans, and a tensor-classification paper blames ambiguous category definitions ([arXiv 2103.08074](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08074), [arXiv 1910.02150](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.02150), both snippet level).

The label-noise evidence is limited and cuts against a pure-noise explanation. A Cleanlab company audit flags more than 100 Shirt-labelled images as probably T-shirt/top and more than 40 as Dress, and argues that "T-shirt/top" covers both t-shirts and top-like shirts, so the Shirt label is inconsistent by definition; it is a company blog, not peer reviewed, and gives no train/test split ([Cleanlab](https://cleanlab.ai/blog/learn/csa/csa-4/)). An independent check of 1,000 samples found 7 hard ones (0.7%) ([arXiv 2102.04266](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.04266)). My own arithmetic (not sourced): 100+ flags over 70,000 images is well under 1%, so noise alone cannot explain a 4-10 point Shirt recall loss; the overlap is systematic. The Northcutt et al. test-set label error study found an average of about 3.3% across ten datasets, but I found no evidence that Fashion-MNIST was one of them ([ar5iv 2103.14749](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2103.14749)).

What helps is largely untested. The only documented lever is augmentation: the top four table entries use crops and flips, and Random Erasing adds about 0.4 point (WRN-28-10 from 95.9% to 96.3%) ([README](https://github.com/zalandoresearch/fashion-mnist)); there is no per-class Shirt effect shown. Hierarchical CNNs that treat tops as a coarse group and refine in a second stage report aggregate gains with no per-class numbers ([Ewha record](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/hierarchical-convolutional-neural-networks-for-fashion-image-clas/)). A 2024 MDPI paper claims 99.65% Fashion-MNIST accuracy with a 3-conv CNN, far above the 96.7% table maximum; it was not read and should be treated as an outlier possibly caused by tuning on the test set ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/12/20/3174)). Specialist networks, mixup, test-time augmentation, ensembles and higher resolution have no sourced numbers, so for this repo the right approach is to run them and print confusion matrices. I found no evidence on whether colour or high-resolution clothing datasets separate Shirt from T-shirt better.

## A credible pitch needs three baseline tiers and equal-byte continual learning

The decisive framing is the one MLPerf Tiny uses: meet a quality target first, then compare latency and energy, with the measurement boundary stated. MLPerf Tiny's image task is CIFAR-10 with a roughly 96 KB ResNet and an **85% top-1 target**; energy is optional and measured in a separate setup ([MLPerf Tiny](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.07597), [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2106.07597)). A Fashion-MNIST or MNIST result therefore is not an MLPerf Tiny result, and a chip that only does MNIST should say so. Tier one is accuracy baselines: the 92.5% tiny CNN under 100K parameters, HOG+SVM at 92.6%, a 88.33% MLP, and WRN40-4 at 96.7% as the unconstrained reference ([Fashion-MNIST table](https://github.com/zalandoresearch/fashion-mnist)). Tier two is chip baselines. The closest peer is a 65 nm all-digital Tsetlin machine at **8.6 nJ per frame and 97.42% MNIST** at 27.8 MHz; other tiny-chip points are a 130 nm in-SRAM classifier at 633 pJ and above 95% MNIST, and a 28 nm sparse DNN engine at 98.36% and 0.36 uJ minimum ([TM chip](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19347v2), [Princeton](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/a-machine-learning-classifier-implemented-in-a-standard-6t-sram-a/), [IBM](https://www.research.ibm.com/publications/dnn-engine-a-28-nm-timing-error-tolerant-sparse-deep-neural-network-processor-for-iot-applications)); all are second-hand listings. No Fashion-MNIST chip energy figure was found, which is both a gap and an opening. System-level MLPerf Tiny energy is far higher (Syntiant keyword spotting 31.5-49.6 uJ; Cortex-M4 ResNet-8 about 8.9 mJ per frame) and includes the MCU and memory, so a bare-core nJ figure is not like-for-like ([EE Asia](https://www.eetasia.com/syntiant-leads-tinyml-benchmark-results/), [arXiv 2609.11939](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11939)). Literal Labs claims 52x lower energy than neural nets on a microcontroller benchmark, which is a company claim on a different platform ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/50x-faster-50x-thriftier-uk-ai-startup-backed-by-arm-delivers-stunning-gains-in-performance-and-power-consumption-using-a-cheap-usd30-system-board)); do not mix it with ASIC numbers.

The "why SRAM" argument rests on Horowitz's 45 nm table as reproduced in patents: 8-bit add 0.03 pJ, 8-bit multiply 0.2 pJ, 32-bit SRAM read about 5 pJ and 32-bit DRAM read about 640 pJ ([patent 12632704](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12632704), [patent 11972137](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11972137)). The original PDF was not readable and no newer-node table was found, so label these as 2014, 45 nm and second-hand. SRAM-centric competitors (Groq 230 MB SRAM, Graphcore 900 MB, Cerebras 44 GB) are vendor figures and still use dense matrix units, so the comparison with a matrix-free chip is energy per task and bytes, not TOPS/W ([Groq brief](https://groq.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/GroqChip™-Processor-Product-Brief-v1.7.pdf), [Cerebras](https://www.cerebras.ai/press-release/cerebras-announces-third-generation-wafer-scale-engine)). The sustainability framing is the IEA's 415 TWh for data centres in 2024 (about 1.5% of world electricity) rising to about 945 TWh in 2030 ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)); the water and e-waste figures vary by paper version and scenario and should be quoted with their version ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271v2)).

Continual-learning baselines must be fair, which means equal bytes and the right scenario. On Split-MNIST, van de Ven et al. report class-incremental accuracy of about 20% for EWC, Online EWC and SI, 23.85% for LwF, 90.79% for generative replay and 94.57% for iCaRL with a 2,000-sample budget, against 19.90% for naive fine-tuning; a later study with 200 stored samples per task reports 85.9% for replay ([van de Ven](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.07734), [arXiv 2603.07415](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.07415), second-hand, table text partly garbled). Beating EWC or SI therefore proves nothing in the class-incremental setting; the credible comparison is replay (and generative or "dream" replay) with the same memory in MB, plus fine-tuning as the lower bound and joint training as a flagged upper reference ([CL survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08383)). Report average accuracy and forgetting per scenario (task-, domain- or class-incremental), and sweep buffer budgets. No typical Split-Fashion-MNIST or Split-CIFAR numbers were retrieved, and no explicit reviewer checklist was found; the guidance above is inferred from how those papers report.

## What a small team should build next

The evidence argues for a staged plan that targets MNIST and Fashion-MNIST first and treats CIFAR-10 as a stretch goal. First, get a trustworthy Fashion-MNIST baseline in-house: reproduce a convolutional TM (pyTsetlinMachine or PyTsetlinMachineCUDA, which already reach about 90-91% with published scripts), a DWN run from its repo, and a tiny CNN under 100K parameters at 92.5%, all with the same evaluation script, five or more seeds and a printed confusion matrix. Second, test the one hypothesis that could make a counting or lookup readout competitive: a fixed HOG-like oriented-edge, spatially pooled bit feature bank feeding a TM or table readout, compared against a thermometer-encoded flat baseline; HOG+SVM at 92.6% is the number to chase, and the notes call this untested. Third, run Shirt-specific experiments: per-class recall and Shirt/T-shirt/Pullover/Coat confusion for every model, a second-stage specialist for the four upper-body classes, Random Erasing and crop augmentation, and a Cleanlab-style relabel check on the test split to measure how much of the gap is definitional. Fourth, measure what a pitch needs but no source supplies: model bytes (clauses or table entries, in KB), operation counts and a modelled or simulated energy per inference with the boundary stated, then place it against the 8.6 nJ Tsetlin chip. Fifth, add the continual-learning protocol: Split-MNIST and Split-Fashion class-incremental, replay at equal bytes, fine-tuning and joint training bounds, and EWC/SI only as weak references. Only after those steps would a CIFAR-10 attempt (convolutional TM composites or a logic-gate tree network) make sense, and it should be reported honestly with gate or clause count next to accuracy, because 61M gates is a different scale from an SRAM-lookup design.

## Conclusion

The notes change the question from "can matrix-free models match CNNs" to "on which dataset, at what gate or byte count, and against which baseline." The honest answer today is that parity is plausible on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST against tiny CNNs (about 92.5%), supported by README-level evidence for the convolutional TM at about 90-91% and snippet-level evidence at 92.5-94.7%, while parity against full-size GPU CNNs (96.7%) and on CIFAR-10 (85% MLPerf Tiny target) is not demonstrated for any lookup-only design that fits a small SRAM. The most promising untested idea is cheap: a fixed oriented-edge bit feature bank with a counting readout, motivated by HOG+SVM matching a CNN with no backpropagation. Shirt versus T-shirt looks like a definitional ambiguity that a specialist upper-body stage and per-class reporting can partly address, and a pitch that quotes class-level numbers will be more credible than one that quotes only the overall figure.

A pitch should also not hide the weak spots. No energy number exists for Fashion-MNIST on any chip, which leaves room to be first but means the claim must be modelled and labelled as such. The continual-learning story only counts if it beats replay at equal bytes, since regularisation baselines sit near 20% in the class-incremental setting. And because nearly every number here came from search summaries rather than tables, the first week of work should be spent re-reading the primary sources in the table above before any figure appears on a slide.
